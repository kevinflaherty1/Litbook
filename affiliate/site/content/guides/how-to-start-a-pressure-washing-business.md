Pressure washing is one of the cheapest service businesses to start. You can begin
with a few thousand dollars of equipment, no storefront, and no special state trade
license in most places. The hard parts are pricing correctly, staying insured, and
finding customers. This guide walks through each step in order.

> **Quick version:** register the business → get liability insurance → buy equipment
> sized to the jobs you'll sell → set prices (use our
> [price calculator](/tools/pressure-washing-price-calculator)) → set up quoting and
> invoicing → get your first 10 customers locally.

## Step 1: Decide what you'll offer first

Don't try to do everything on day one. Most new operators start with residential work:

| Service | Why start with it | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Driveways, patios and sidewalks (concrete) | Easy to learn, dramatic before/after photos | A surface cleaner attachment makes it much faster |
| House washing (soft wash) | High demand, good prices | Uses low pressure plus cleaning solution, not high pressure, to avoid damaging siding |
| Decks and fences | Good add-on to house washes | Wood is easy to gouge with too much pressure |
| Roof soft washing | Highest price per sq ft | Highest risk; leave it until you have experience and the right insurance |

Starting with concrete and house washing keeps your equipment list short.

## Step 2: Register your business

Most states don't have a special license for pressure washing. What you typically need
is ordinary business registration plus any local business license. In practice that means:

1. **Choose a structure.** A sole proprietorship is simplest. Many owners form an LLC
   to separate business and personal liability. Talk to an accountant if you're unsure.
2. **Register your business name** with your state (or file a DBA if you operate under a
   name that isn't your own).
3. **Get an EIN** (a free federal tax ID) from the IRS website. You'll need it for a
   business bank account and for hiring later.
4. **Get a local business license** from your city or county, if required.
5. **Open a separate business bank account.**

**State rules differ. Check yours.** For example, California lets unlicensed workers do
"minor work" only up to **$1,000** per job (raised from $500 on January 1, 2025), and only
if the job needs no building permit and you don't hire workers
([CSLB bulletin](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Resources/IndustryBulletins/2024/AB%202622%20Implementation.FINAL.pdf)).
Some cities also have rules about wastewater from washing, especially commercial jobs.
Washwater generally isn't allowed to flow into storm drains
([UNC stormwater guidance](https://ehs.unc.edu/stormwater/pollution-prevention/pressure-washing/)).
Call your city's business licensing office and ask what applies to exterior cleaning.

## Step 3: Get insured before your first job

**General liability insurance** is the one policy you shouldn't skip. It covers you if you
damage a customer's property or someone gets hurt. A common recommendation is $1–2 million
of coverage, which is commonly quoted at about **$500–$1,500 a year** for a small operator. Get quotes
from two or three insurers, because prices vary by state and services.

Also consider:

- **Commercial auto** if you use a vehicle for work. Personal auto policies may not cover
  business use.
- **Inland marine / equipment coverage** to protect equipment on a trailer or truck.
- **Workers' comp** once you hire. It's required in most states.

Commercial customers will often ask for a **certificate of insurance** before you start.

## Step 4: Buy equipment (lean is fine)

Published startup cost estimates fall into three rough tiers:

| Setup | Typical cost | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Starter | about $500–$2,500 | A good gas pressure washer, surface cleaner, hoses, nozzles, chemicals, safety gear |
| Professional | about $5,000–$15,000 | Higher-flow machine, soft wash pump system, water tank, better hoses and reels |
| Full commercial rig | about $20,000–$50,000+ | Hot water unit, trailer, large tank, reclaim equipment |

Tips for a lean start:

- **Prioritize flow (GPM), not just pressure (PSI).** Higher flow cleans faster, which
  matters more for a business than a big PSI number.
- **Buy a surface cleaner** for concrete. It cuts driveway time dramatically and leaves
  even results.
- **Consider used equipment**, but inspect the pump and engine carefully.
- **Budget for safety gear**: eye protection, gloves, non-slip boots, and chemical-safe
  clothing for soft washing.

## Step 5: Set your prices

Most residential pressure washing is priced per square foot, with a job minimum.
Typical published 2026 ranges:

| Job | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Concrete (driveways, patios) | about $0.15–$0.35 per sq ft |
| Vinyl siding house wash | about $0.12–$0.35 per sq ft |
| Wood decks | about $0.30–$0.60 per sq ft (some sources go higher) |
| Roof soft wash | about $0.30–$0.75 per sq ft |
| Job minimum | commonly $150–$350 |

## Step 6: Set up quoting, scheduling and invoicing

When you're starting out, you need a simple way to:

1. Send a professional quote quickly (ideally from your phone at the customer's house).
2. Turn an accepted quote into a scheduled job.
3. Send an invoice and take card payments.
4. Remember to follow up for repeat work next year.

You *can* do this with a spreadsheet and a free invoicing app at first. Once you're doing
several jobs a week, field-service software saves real time. The common options for
small exterior cleaning businesses include [Jobber](/go/jobber),
[Housecall Pro](/go/housecall-pro) and [QuoteIQ](/go/quoteiq), all of which offer free
trials. We're testing each one hands-on and will publish detailed reviews. Until then,
take a trial yourself before paying for anything.

## Step 7: Get your first 10 customers

- **Google Business Profile.** Create a free profile so you show up in local map
  results. Ask every happy customer for a review.
- **Neighbors of every job.** After finishing a driveway, leave door hangers on nearby
  houses. The clean driveway is your advertisement.
- **Before and after photos.** Take them on every job. They're your best marketing on
  Facebook, Nextdoor and your website.
- **Local Facebook and Nextdoor groups.** Follow each group's rules about promotion.
- **Partner with related businesses** such as realtors (homes going on the market),
  property managers and painters.
- **Offer recurring service.** An annual house wash or twice-yearly concrete cleaning
  turns one-time customers into steady income.

## Step 8: Track the numbers that matter

From the first job, track your revenue per job, time per job, and costs (fuel, chemicals,
equipment wear). A commonly cited target is an effective rate of **$150–$300 per hour**
of on-site work once you're experienced.
If you're well below that, raise prices or speed up with better equipment.
